# Copy Framework

Pulled from Harry Dry's "Learn Copywriting in 76 Minutes" (youtube.com/watch?v=TUMjnmfsPeM), then turned into checks for the launch video. The second half scores [script-draft-8.md](script-draft-8.md) against it.

## 1. The Three Rules, Every Line

Ask these of each sentence. Three no's means filler. Three yes's means you're onto something.

- **Can I visualize it?** If you can't picture it, nobody remembers it. "Charging pitbull" sticks and "seamless transition" doesn't. Concrete is something you could drop on your foot.
- **Can I falsify it?** A line that's true or false makes people sit up, because you've put your head on the block. "Good values" is just talk. "Reads on the tube" can be checked.
- **Can nobody else say it?** Never write a line a competitor could sign. If another startup could read your sentence out word for word, it isn't yours yet.

## 2. Before Writing: A to B

- **Start from the viewer's current attitude and end at the one you want.** You can't start the race in the middle.
- **Who are you talking to?** The person, where they are, how much attention they're giving. (Snapchat bought a Super Bowl ad for an audience whose average age is 39.)
- **Have something to say.** A belief, something you think is wrong with the world. Copy gets easy when there's a conviction under it.
- **Then say it well.** Most people think this is all of copywriting. It's the last third.

## 3. Tools for Writing the Line

- **Zoom in.** Write the abstract word at the top of the page and keep asking "what do I actually mean?" until you hit an object. "Regain fitness" → "off the couch" → "run 5K" → *Couch to 5K*.
- **Don't talk, point.** You can't describe, you can only point at something real: the chart, the number, the thing on screen. It gets you off the adjective trail.
- **If in doubt, give a fact.** A fact guarantees you're saying something. Most talk is "word-shaped air". Facts don't have to be numbers: something you saw happen is a fact.
- **Find the enemy.** Every good example hinges on a "but", without writing "but". Three kinds of enemy: a different approach, a different belief, a competitor. We judge things relative to each other, so show the old way next to the new one.
- **Dividing lines and parallelism.** Split the big thing into two or three parts with the same shape. "Tougher than an F-150, faster than a 911." "22,000 hours writing, two learning how."
- **Show what it isn't.** Positioning 101: what does it replace?
- **Swap the literal word for a visual one.** "A thousand songs in your *pocket*", not "in your media player".
- **First line, second line.** The only job of a line is to get the next one read.
- **Small, sincere claims beat big ones.** "From 1% to 2%" is believable. "Become a millionaire" isn't.
- **Make the viewer feel smart** for picking you.

## 4. Tools for the Edit

- **Kaplan's law of words.** Any word that isn't working for you is working against you. Same for ideas: the strength of an idea shrinks as its scope grows, which is why "and" is a warning sign.
- **The burrito test.** A good paragraph breaks if you pull a sentence out. If it still works without the sentence, cut the sentence.
- **Write simply by rewriting.** Keep four or five versions of a line side by side. Show people versions, not one draft, and the feedback gets sharp: "that sentence, in that one".
- **Write it where it'll be seen.** Harry writes ads in Figma and newsletters in the newsletter tool. For us: read each line out loud over the frame it plays on, at 1.3x.
- **One Mississippi, two Mississippi.** Each beat should land in two seconds of looking.
- **The garden walk.** Come back to it. If you can't add or remove a word and make it better, it's done. If it isn't a 9 out of 10, it doesn't ship.

## 5. What AI Can't Write

Taste, conviction, and experience. Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce line came from someone who sat in the car. Bukowski delivered mail for years and wrote *Post Office*. AI predicts from what's already been written, so the lines only you can write come from what only you have done.

For this video that's the argument itself: the script says vision and taste can't be cloned. The script should sound like it can't be cloned either.

## Draft 8 Against the Framework

### A to B for This Video

- **Who:** hackathon judges at Meta, Push the Frontier track, watching a lot of demos back to back. Later, creators and builders on the site.
- **A, where they start:** "AI is eating SaaS. This is probably another app builder on top of an agent."
- **B, where they should end:** "Agents make a new kind of software possible: code you own that still gets updates. Muse Code is the piece that makes it work. And this person has built platforms before."
- **The one thing to say:** software should be installed and owned, not cloned, and agents are what finally make that work.

Draft 8 gets them most of the way to B, but the last two parts of B are weak, and the "probably another app builder" doubt never gets punched out.

### Why It Feels Flat

1. **Nobody is talking.** The one line no one else can say is gone: Dylan co-founded ClickFunnels, his own products are getting cloned, and he spent eight months fighting it before building for it. It was in draft 1 and the beats, and drafts 3–8 cut it. A script arguing that vision and taste can't be copied currently sounds like it could be anyone's. (Dylan chose to keep the narrator anonymous, so draft 9 makes up for this in the product lines instead.)
2. **The enemy shows up late and soft.** The hook's conflict is "AI vs SaaS", which is abstract. The concrete enemy, someone handing an agent their login and cloning a product, doesn't arrive until line 9, and then as "building is becoming cloning".
3. **Talking instead of pointing.** A lot of word-shaped air: *breaking the business model, expensive, demanding, fragile, vision, taste, constraints, opinions, ownership, seamlessly, small product, real business*. None of these can be pictured.
4. **The frontier claim is told, not argued.** The sharpest dividing line in the whole idea, *own the code or get the updates, never both*, is never said in those words. And the reason it works, Muse Code made the changes so it can merge around them, was in the beats and is missing from draft 8.
5. **Lists where one point should be.** The app capabilities sentence has eight items. The ending has three separate endings. Each list makes every item weaker.
6. **Sentences that restate.** These pass the burrito test, which means they can go: "That makes every app a small product…", "It's what makes owning the code possible in this new SaaS model", "SaaS isn't going away, but it is changing".
7. **One rhythm.** Almost every sentence is mid-length and shaped "X used to…, now…" or "X, so Y". No short lines that get room to land.

### The Three Moves That Matter Most

1. **Put the enemy in the first ten seconds, in a picture.** Someone hands an agent their login and gets the product back.
2. **Make every line one only SuperGrid could say.** Dylan decided (2026-11-03) to keep the narrator anonymous, so the "nobody else can say it" test has to be passed by the product lines instead: your repo, your Cloudflare account, your Muse subscription, and merges around your changes.
3. **Give the update beat a dividing line and a reason.** "Own the code or get the updates, never both." Then: Muse Code can merge around your changes because it made them. And show it on screen, which the beats already asked for.
